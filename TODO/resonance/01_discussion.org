** What Rob's rig actually is
*** A feed-forward bank of biquads, no string-to-string feedback
=src/sympathetics.rs= in rfielding/maqam is up to 64 two-pole
resonators, =y = 2r cos(w) y1 - r^2 y2 + drive=, one per partial
of the current phrase's just pitches (plus the 4/3 and 3/2 over
the tonic and their harmonics). "Just add the sound coming in to
each string" is literally the algorithm: the same input,
tanh-clipped and gated, is added into every resonator. Nothing
goes from any resonator back into any other or back into the
input. So his bank is stable for the trivial reason that every
pole radius is below 1; there is no coupling problem to solve,
and also no mutual resonance. The pitches come from the score,
not from pitch-tracking the oud.
*** The Marshall stack was a Marshall stack
His =dgrowl.mq= preset runs the oud through a Neural Amp Modeler
capture of a JCM800 2203X before the strings ever hear it, and

** Bodies, since you asked
A body does three separable jobs and each belongs in a different
place in the graph.
*** The bridge admittance
What the strings feel at their end. It sets per-partial damping,
pulls partials slightly, and is the path by which strings couple:
it must be live, inside the loop, low-order and passive.
*** The radiation
Bridge force to sound. It is what gives "wood" or "soundboard"
colour: it is high-order and belongs on the summed output, as a
partitioned convolution with a measured or synthetic impulse
response or as 30 to 50 parallel biquads, costing the same at any
polyphony.
*** The knock
The body's own response to being hit. It belongs in the
excitation, commuted into the pluck or strike table.
*** Bank and Karjalainen's guitar
Exactly this split: a 60th-order passive admittance in the loop,
two 200th-order radiation filters on the output.
*** The rule both they and Smith follow
Never put the long impulse response inside the loop, for three
reasons:
**** A fitted high-order response is generally not passive
**** One slightly active mode makes a partial grow forever
**** Block-based convolution adds a block of delay
Any block-based convolution would add a block of delay into every
string loop.
*** McPherson's note: why do the in-loop part at all
It comes from the real magnetic resonator piano: a sustained note
with the dampers down "sounds flat and synthetic" next to the
same note with the other strings free to ring.

** Coupling every key to every key
*** The right object: a shared bridge junction, not an NxN matrix
Smith's loaded series junction, with all strings of equal
impedance and a bridge impedance ratio =r=: =v_b = 2/(N + r) *
sum_i v_i_in=, then each string gets back =v_i_out = v_b -
v_i_in=. At =r = 0= this is the Householder matrix, orthogonal
and lossless for any N and any delay lengths (Schlecht and Habets
2017), which matters because your N and your pitches change
constantly. At =r > 0= it is strictly passive and =r= acts as a
"bridge heaviness" knob: large =r= gives weak, fixed-per-pair
coupling (the piano regime), small =r= gives strong coupling that
renormalizes itself as notes come and go. It costs O(N) per
sample: one sum, one multiply, N subtractions. Adding a few
passive bridge modes to the =2/(N+r)= scalar is what turns
"two-stage decay only" into "two-stage decay plus beating plus
partial-dependent damping".
*** What does not work
**** Adding c times everyone's output into everyone's input
Simply adding it has an in-phase eigenvalue of =1 + c(N-1)=,
always above 1; in the simulation it diverged immediately at c =
1/N and c = 1/sqrt(N) and was stable only near c = 1e-5, which is
inaudible.
**** Delaying the cross terms by one audio buffer
This also diverged (the in-phase gain approaches 3), so the
junction must run per sample or in micro-blocks no longer than
the shortest sounding period, about 8 to 11 samples at the top of
the piano.
**** 32 coupled strings computed per sample
They ran at about one percent of one core, so the cpal buffer
only sets control latency.
*** The howl is a separate, deliberately active path
The howl path is a chain whose result is added back into every
string:
**** Sum of all strings
**** Gain
**** A tanh or cubic clipper
The clipper bounds it for any gain and any delay, so this is the
one place a whole buffer of latency is harmless.
**** A delay of 1 to 10 ms
This is the speaker distance.
**** Above threshold a note sustains forever
The partials that several notes share (unisons, octaves, fifths)
win.
**** Caution: keep it out of the passive junction
That keeps the two concerns independent.
**** Caution: Fletcher-style locking fights EDO tuning
It pulls near-unisons and near-octaves toward just ratios while
the loop saturates, which fights deliberate EDO tuning; that
follows from the literature and nobody has listened to it in 41-
or 58-EDO.

** Continuous energy: how the pedal becomes a bow
*** The simplest bloom is an EBow, not a bow
Feed each string's own output back into itself through a soft
limiter: =drive = k * A * tanh(v / A)=. The string's per-period
gain becomes =g + k=. Below =k = 1 - g= the pedal merely
lengthens sustain; above it the note grows exponentially from
whatever energy it has (a pluck, sympathetic ring, or a tiny
noise floor) until tanh flattens it at about A. That is "energy
ramps in, string blooms", with no non-starts, no squeaks, and one
knob. The reviewers' key suggestion for feel: map the pedal to a
growth rate in nepers per second and derive k from it per note,
=k = exp(rate / f0) - g=, so a given pedal position feels the
same in every register; let A rise with the pedal too so more
pedal is both faster and louder.
*** Bow and wind are the richer, twitchier alternatives
**** A friction-table bow
Examples: Smith's waveguide bow, STK Bowed, Elements' banded
waveguides. It gives rosin bite and a locked sawtooth spectrum
but only inside the Schelleng window of force versus velocity;
expect squeaks and non-starts at extremes.
**** Wind (the aeolian harp)
Bandpassed noise centred at the Strouhal frequency, so the
partial that blooms climbs the series as the pedal rises.
**** Elements has all three exciters in MIT C++
I would build the EBow first and keep the exciter as a plug so
bow and wind can follow.

****** [Claude] good: the integral is cheap, and no latency
Nothing needs to run for an unkeyed string. The runtime keeps
one ring buffer of the pedal's position at control rate (say
every millisecond), as long as =ex-ante-energizing-ms=. At
key-down it computes the energy that string would hold now,
fills the new string with that much energy in a plausible
waveshape, and the live drive carries on for the ex-post ramp.
Three parts to that:
******* The integral
Energy put in at time t decays before key-down, so the amount
left is a weighted sum over the window: each history sample
contributes =triangle(t) * pedal(t)^exponent=, times
=exp(-(T - t) / tau)=, where =tau= is that string's own decay
time (from its T60 at its pitch). A window of two seconds at
one millisecond is two thousand multiply-adds per key-down, a
cost that does not register. Done in amplitude rather than
energy, that sum is exactly what a linear string would hold
had it been driven the whole time at that rate.
******* Why the pre-charge must be additive
The EBow drive proposed above multiplies what is already
there, so a silent string would never gather anything. For
the pre-charge, and I think for the live drive too, model the
bow energy as a source: each sample adds =k(t)= worth of the
drive waveform regardless of the string's state, through the
same soft limiter so it cannot run away. Your "the string is
moving but the mic is off" is literally that. It also makes
the pre-charge integral exact rather than an approximation.
******* The shortcut about harmonics
The waveshape to fill the string with is the steady state a
continuously driven string settles into. For an EBow that is
nearly a sine at the fundamental with weak harmonics; for a
bow it is the Helmholtz sawtooth, lowpassed by the string's
own loss; for wind it is the one partial nearest the Strouhal
frequency. Filling the delay line with one period of that
shape, scaled by the integral, is the "not too wrong"
shortcut: the harmonics are those the drive would have left,
and the string's own loss filter trims them from the first
period onward. The harness can A/B these fills against a
string that really was driven for the whole window.

***** TODO jeff : intereesting
Does this have a physical analog in the piano?
The detuning is obvious to me, but the difference in decay is not.
****** [Claude] yes: Weinreich's coupled strings, polarization
A piano note has two or three unison strings on one bridge.
The strings themselves have the same loss; the difference in
decay belongs to the coupled modes, not to either string. When
the unisons move in phase they push the bridge together, lose
energy into it fast, and give the loud "prompt sound". When
they move in antiphase the bridge barely moves, so that mode
decays slowly and gives the "aftersound". The slight detuning
is what keeps trading energy between the two modes, which is
the beating. Weinreich measured this in 1977 and it is the
accepted explanation of the piano's two-stage decay. The other
analog is within one string: motion toward the soundboard
couples strongly to the bridge and dies fast, motion parallel
to it couples weakly and lingers.
******* What this means for the model
The "one decays two to four times slower" shortcut was for
two uncoupled loops. With the junction present it is wrong to
do it by hand: give both unisons the same loss, detune them a
little, and the bridge produces the two decay rates and the
beating on its own. The hand-set version stays as a cheap
fallback if the junction is ever off.
