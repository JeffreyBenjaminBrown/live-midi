#+title: Slide math: guitar-style pitch ramps, and Hz ↔ pitch
#+author: notes

* The short answer

A guitar slide is *linear in period*, not linear in Hz and not linear in pitch.

With the ramp normalized to \(t \in [0,1]\), start \(f_0\), end \(f_1\):

\[
f(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{\dfrac{1-t}{f_0} + \dfrac{t}{f_1}}
\;=\; \frac{f_0 f_1}{f_1 + t\,(f_0 - f_1)}
\]

That is the harmonic (reciprocal) interpolation between \(f_0\) and \(f_1\), as
against the arithmetic interpolation \((1-t)f_0 + t f_1\) that a Hz-linear ramp
uses.

Hz gives the simpler closed form — it is a ratio of linear functions, with no
transcendentals. But the *simplest* dependent variable is neither Hz nor pitch:
it is the period \(T = 1/f\), where the ramp is just a lerp,

\[
T(t) = (1-t)\,T_0 + t\,T_1 .
\]

Everything below is downstream of that one line.

* Derivation

** The string

For an ideal string of vibrating length \(L\), tension \(\tau\), linear density \(\mu\):

\[
f = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\mu}} = \frac{v}{2L}
\]

Sliding a finger (or a bottleneck) along the neck changes \(L\) and leaves
\(\tau\) and \(\mu\) alone, so \(v\) is constant and

\[
f \propto \frac{1}{L}.
\]

** Constant slide velocity

"The string length changes linearly" means the hand moves at constant speed:

\[
L(t) = (1-t)\,L_0 + t\,L_1 .
\]

Substitute \(L_i = v / (2 f_i)\):

\[
f(t) = \frac{v}{2\big[(1-t)L_0 + t L_1\big]}
     = \frac{v}{2\Big[(1-t)\frac{v}{2f_0} + t\frac{v}{2f_1}\Big]}
     = \frac{1}{\dfrac{1-t}{f_0} + \dfrac{t}{f_1}} .
\]

The \(v\) cancels, which is why the result depends only on the endpoints and not
on the physical scale of the instrument. Clearing the compound fraction:

\[
\boxed{\;f(t) = \frac{f_0 f_1}{(1-t)\,f_1 + t\,f_0} = \frac{f_0 f_1}{f_1 + t\,(f_0-f_1)}\;}
\]

Check: \(f(0) = f_0f_1/f_1 = f_0\); \(f(1) = f_0f_1/f_0 = f_1\). ✓

** Read it in the period

Take reciprocals of the first form:

\[
\frac{1}{f(t)} = \frac{1-t}{f_0} + \frac{t}{f_1}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
T(t) = (1-t)T_0 + t T_1 .
\]

So the whole content of "guitar-style" is: *the period ramps linearly*.
Equivalently the wavelength \(\lambda = v/f \propto L\) ramps linearly — which is
just restating that the hand moves at constant speed. The three descriptions
(linear length, linear wavelength, linear period) are the same statement.

** In pitch

Let \(p\) be pitch in semitones, \(p = p_{\text{ref}} + 12\log_2(f/f_{\text{ref}})\). Then

\[
p(t) = p_0 + 12\log_2\!\frac{f(t)}{f_0}
     = p_0 - 12\log_2\!\Big[(1-t) + t\,\frac{f_0}{f_1}\Big]
\]

and writing \(\Delta = p_1 - p_0\) for the total interval in semitones, so that
\(f_0/f_1 = 2^{-\Delta/12}\):

\[
\boxed{\;p(t) = p_0 - 12\log_2\!\Big[1 - t\big(1 - 2^{-\Delta/12}\big)\Big]\;}
\]

Sanity: \(t=0\) gives \(p_0\); \(t=1\) gives \(p_0 - 12\log_2 2^{-\Delta/12} = p_0 + \Delta = p_1\). ✓

Hz is the simpler of the two. So: *use the Hz form*, or better, lerp the
period and take one reciprocal.

* Why it is the reverse of a Hz-linear ramp

The thing you actually hear is \(dp/dt\), the glide rate in semitones per unit
time. For any ramp, \(\frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{12}{\ln 2}\cdot\frac{f'(t)}{f(t)}\).

** Hz-linear

\(f(t) = f_0 + t(f_1-f_0)\), so \(f'\) is constant and

\[
\frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{12}{\ln 2}\,\frac{f_1-f_0}{f(t)} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{f}.
\]

Fast through low pitches, slow through high ones. Confirms the premise.

** Guitar

\(f = 1/T\) with \(T\) linear, so \(f' = -T'/T^2 = -(T_1-T_0)\,f^2\), giving
\(f'/f = (T_0-T_1)\,f\) and

\[
\frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{12}{\ln 2}\Big(\frac{1}{f_0}-\frac{1}{f_1}\Big) f(t) \;\propto\; f.
\]

Exactly inverted. Sliding *up* the neck at constant speed, you cross semitones
faster and faster — which is the same fact as frets getting closer together as
you climb. The fretboard's geometry *is* this formula.

** The family that contains both

Both are members of the power-mean interpolation

\[
f_s(t) = \Big[(1-t)\,f_0^{\,s} + t\,f_1^{\,s}\Big]^{1/s},
\qquad s\in\mathbb{R},\; s=0\text{ by limit},
\]

for which \(\dfrac{dp}{dt} = \dfrac{12}{\ln 2}\cdot\dfrac{f_1^{\,s}-f_0^{\,s}}{s}\cdot f^{-s} \;\propto\; f^{-s}\).

| \(s\)  | ramp                | \(f(t)\)                     | midpoint \(f(\tfrac12)\)        | glide rate    |
|--------+---------------------+------------------------------+---------------------------------+---------------|
| \(+1\) | linear in Hz        | \((1-t)f_0 + tf_1\)          | arithmetic \(\frac{f_0+f_1}{2}\) | \(\propto 1/f\) |
| \(0\)  | linear in pitch     | \(f_0 (f_1/f_0)^t\)          | geometric \(\sqrt{f_0 f_1}\)     | constant      |
| \(-1\) | linear in period ⇒ /guitar/ | \(\big[\frac{1-t}{f_0}+\frac{t}{f_1}\big]^{-1}\) | harmonic \(\frac{2f_0f_1}{f_0+f_1}\) | \(\propto f\) |

AM ≥ GM ≥ HM, so an upward guitar slide always sits *below* the exponential
glide: it hangs near the low note and then bolts for the high one.

** An exact duality

For every \(s\) and every \(t\),

\[
f_s(t)\cdot f_{-s}(1-t) = f_0 f_1,
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
p_s(t) + p_{-s}(1-t) = p_0 + p_1 .
\]

/Proof./ \(f_{-s}(1-t)^{-s} = t f_0^{-s} + (1-t) f_1^{-s} = \dfrac{(1-t)f_0^{\,s} + t f_1^{\,s}}{(f_0f_1)^{s}} = \dfrac{f_s(t)^{\,s}}{(f_0f_1)^{s}}\).
Rearrange and take the \(1/s\) power. ∎

Setting \(s=1\): the guitar ramp is the Hz-linear ramp *run backwards and
reflected through the geometric mean*. The exponential ramp \(s=0\) is the fixed
point of that symmetry — which is why it looks like the "neutral" glide.

This is the user's intuition ("on a guitar the reverse happens") stated exactly:
not merely qualitatively opposite, but the precise involution \(s \mapsto -s\).

* Worked example: A2 → A4 (110 → 440 Hz, two octaves)

Semitones are measured above A2.

| \(t\)  | guitar Hz | guitar st | pitch-linear Hz | st | Hz-linear Hz | st |
|--------+-----------+-----------+-----------------+----+--------------+----|
| 0.00   |   110.000 |     0.000 |         110.000 |  0 |      110.000 |  0.000 |
| 0.25   |   135.385 |     3.595 |         155.563 |  6 |      192.500 |  9.688 |
| 0.50   |   176.000 |     8.137 |         220.000 | 12 |      275.000 | 15.863 |
| 0.75   |   251.429 |    14.312 |         311.127 | 18 |      357.500 | 20.405 |
| 1.00   |   440.000 |    24.000 |         440.000 | 24 |      440.000 | 24.000 |

Halfway through the slide you are only 8.14 of 24 semitones up — a third of the
way musically, in half the time. The Hz-linear ramp is at 15.86, and
\(8.137 + 15.863 = 24\): the duality above, at \(t=\tfrac12\), where it reads
\(\mathrm{HM}\cdot\mathrm{AM} = \mathrm{GM}^2 = f_0f_1\).

* Implementation

** Per-sample

Lerp the period; reciprocate once.

#+begin_src rust
// t in [0,1]
let t0 = 1.0 / f0;
let t1 = 1.0 / f1;
let f  = 1.0 / (t0 + (t1 - t0) * t);   // guitar
#+end_src

Numerically this is well-behaved everywhere: \(T(t)\) is a convex combination of
two positive numbers, so it never approaches zero and the reciprocal never blows
up. (The Hz-linear ramp has the same property. The naive "lerp in Hz then
convert to pitch" is where people get bitten, not here.)

** Exact accumulated phase

Driving an oscillator, phase in *cycles* over a ramp of real duration \(D\)
seconds is \(\Phi(t) = D\int_0^t f(u)\,du\). For the guitar ramp this integrates
in closed form:

\[
\Phi(t) = \frac{D}{T_1 - T_0}\,\ln\!\frac{T(t)}{T_0},
\qquad T(t) = T_0 + (T_1-T_0)t .
\]

Guard the degenerate case: as \(T_1 \to T_0\) this tends to \(f_0 D t\), so branch
on \(|T_1 - T_0| < \varepsilon\).

Checked against a 48 kHz sample-by-sample accumulator over 110→440 Hz in 2 s:
both give 406.6463 cycles (agreement to \(10^{-8}\)). You only need the closed
form if you must seek to arbitrary \(t\) without replaying the ramp; a running
accumulator is otherwise fine.

*Aside.* The mean frequency over the ramp, \(\Phi(1)/D\), is the reciprocal of
the *logarithmic mean* of the two periods. (For an exponential glide it is the
logarithmic mean of the two frequencies. Pleasant, and of no practical use.)

** Caveats about actual guitars

- This is a *slide* — bottleneck, or a fretless neck — at constant hand speed.
  Fretted playing quantizes the same curve to \(L_n = L_0\,2^{-n/12}\), and those
  fret positions are spaced logarithmically *because* length is not linear in
  pitch. The fretboard is the \(s=0\) ramp; the slide is the \(s=-1\) one.
- A *bend* is a different law. It changes tension, and \(f \propto \sqrt{\tau/\mu}\);
  the map from finger displacement to tension is itself nonlinear. Same for a
  whammy bar. Do not model a bend with this formula.
- A trombone slide and a slide whistle change acoustic length the same way, so
  they obey the identical \(s=-1\) ramp. Tape varispeed gives \(f \propto\) tape
  speed, so linear spooldown is a Hz-linear ramp (\(s=+1\)). A theremin is
  roughly \(s=0\).
