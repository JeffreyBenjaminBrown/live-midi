#+TITLE: Bases and Generators for the Space of Periodic Waveforms
#+DATE: 2026-04-14

* The problem

Every repeating waveform can be decomposed into sine waves (Fourier's
theorem). But specifying every harmonic individually is tedious.
Meanwhile, the "classic" simple waveforms---square, triangle,
sawtooth---each have a single fixed spectrum (modulo fundamental
frequency). Trapezoid waves add one parameter (the flat-top fraction
=s=, morphing between triangle and square), but that's still not very
flexible.

What other approaches exist for spanning the space of periodic
functions, or interesting subsets of it, with few parameters and cheap
computation?

* Mathematical bases (not just sines)

** Fourier basis (sines and cosines)

The standard. Every L^2 periodic function can be represented as a sum
of sines and cosines. Complete and orthonormal. The downside: you need
infinitely many coefficients to represent anything with sharp features,
and each coefficient is an independent degree of freedom.

** Walsh functions (square-wave basis)

Yes, square waves (or rather, functions that only take values +1 and -1)
can form a complete orthonormal basis. The Walsh functions are exactly
this. They are defined on [0,1) and take only the values +1 and -1,
switching at dyadic rationals. They are ordered by "sequency" (the
analog of frequency---the number of zero-crossings per period).

The Walsh-Hadamard transform is the analog of the Fourier transform in
this basis. All arithmetic is over {+1, -1}, so the transform requires
only additions and subtractions---no multiplies at all. This made Walsh
functions attractive for early digital hardware.

For audio, Walsh functions are less natural than Fourier because they
don't correspond to physical resonances. But they demonstrate that many
different function families can span the same space.

** Haar wavelets

The Haar wavelet is the simplest wavelet: a step function that is +1 on
[0, 0.5) and -1 on [0.5, 1). Its scaled and translated copies form an
orthonormal basis for L^2. Unlike Fourier or Walsh, Haar is /localized/:
each basis function has compact support.

Haar wavelets are closely related to Walsh functions---the Haar system
is the wavelet packet family you get using Walsh/Hadamard filters. But
Haar is localized in time while Walsh is not.

For periodic waveform generation, Haar-like bases suggest an approach:
instead of specifying global harmonics, you specify local features at
different scales.

** Chebyshev polynomials (waveshaping basis)

Chebyshev polynomials T_n(x) = cos(n * arccos(x)) are an orthogonal
basis on [-1, 1] with weight function 1/sqrt(1-x^2). They have a
remarkable property for audio:

If you feed a sine wave of amplitude 1 through a transfer function
defined by the n-th Chebyshev polynomial, you get /exactly/ the n-th
harmonic. A weighted sum of Chebyshev polynomials as a transfer function
therefore produces exactly the corresponding weighted sum of harmonics.

This is the mathematical basis of /waveshaping synthesis/ (see below).
The catch: it only works perfectly for a single sine input at amplitude
exactly 1. Change the input amplitude, and intermodulation products
appear. But this amplitude-dependent behavior is itself musically
interesting---it gives dynamic timbral variation.

* Dynamical systems as oscillators

** The spring-mass system (simple harmonic oscillator)

You're right to remember this. A mass on a spring with no friction:

    m * x'' = -k * x

The solution is x(t) = A * cos(omega * t + phi), where omega =
sqrt(k/m). This is /the/ canonical generator of sine waves. The system
has two state variables (position x, velocity v = x'), and the update
rule is:

    x' = v
    v' = -(k/m) * x

This is a 2D rotation in phase space. Digitize it naively with forward
Euler and you get exponential amplitude growth; digitize it carefully
and you get stable digital oscillators (see below).

** Digital coupled-form oscillators (the magic circle)

The "magic circle" algorithm (modified coupled form, MCF) is a digital
implementation of the spring-mass idea. The state is two numbers
(x1, x2), updated each sample:

    x1(n) = x1(n-1) + epsilon * x2(n-1)
    x2(n) = x2(n-1) - epsilon * x1(n)       [note: uses NEW x1]

where epsilon = 2*sin(pi*f/fs). This is a leapfrog integrator applied
to the harmonic oscillator equations. The "trick" is that x2's update
uses the /already-updated/ x1, which gives error cancellation.

Properties:
- Generates a pure sine wave with no wavetable lookup.
- Only one or two multiplies per sample.
- No exponential amplitude drift from quantization errors (unlike naive
  coupled form where cos^2 + sin^2 may drift from 1).
- Frequency is controlled by a single parameter epsilon.

This is the digital realization of your intuition: "instead of iterating
a complex power series, just iterate a simpler process on a memory."
Two numbers of state, one multiply, one sine wave. The digital waveguide
oscillator (Julius O. Smith, Perry Cook) generalizes this further.

** The Van der Pol oscillator (a nonlinear dynamical system)

The Van der Pol oscillator adds nonlinear damping to the harmonic
oscillator:

    x'' - mu*(1 - x^2)*x' + x = 0

- For mu = 0, it's a simple harmonic oscillator (sine wave).
- For small mu, the output is a slightly distorted sine---a limit cycle
  with gentle nonlinear coloring.
- For large mu, you get /relaxation oscillations/: the waveform
  approaches a square wave, with slow buildup and fast snap transitions.

So a single parameter mu continuously morphs from sine to (approximately)
square. This is more interesting than a trapezoid's parameter, because the
intermediate shapes come from dynamics rather than piecewise linearity.
The waveform has a "natural" quality because it arises from a physical
process (it was first observed in vacuum tube circuits).

Computing it requires numerically integrating the ODE each sample, which
is more expensive than a table lookup but entirely feasible.

** Karplus-Strong (a delay-line dynamical system)

A completely different kind of dynamical system: fill a delay line with
random noise, then feed it back through a simple lowpass filter (e.g.,
average adjacent samples). The result: the noise "crystallizes" into a
pitched tone that sounds like a plucked string.

The system's state is the entire contents of the delay line. The delay
length determines pitch. The filter determines decay rate and timbral
evolution. The initial noise provides richness that decays into
near-sinusoidal behavior---mimicking a real string.

This is physical modeling synthesis in its simplest form: derive the
waveform from a simulated physical process, not from a mathematical
formula.

** Subtractive synthesis (East Coast / Moog)

The classic approach, pioneered by Robert Moog (1964 onward):

1. Start with a harmonically rich waveform (sawtooth, square, pulse).
2. Remove harmonics with a voltage-controlled filter (VCF).
3. Shape amplitude with an envelope generator (ADSR).
4. Modulate filter cutoff with an LFO or envelope for movement.

The filter is the key expressive element. Moog's transistor ladder
filter, for instance, has a distinctive resonant character that defines
the "Moog sound." Different filter topologies (ladder, state-variable,
Sallen-Key, diode) give different timbral characters.

The "basis" here is: {one fixed waveform} x {filter transfer function
space}. The filter cutoff and resonance are the two main parameters.
This is /extremely/ cheap to compute and gives musically useful results.

** DONE nah : Additive synthesis (the direct Fourier approach)

The brute-force version of Fourier: generate individual sine waves for
each harmonic, each with its own amplitude and phase envelope. This gives
total control but requires many oscillators and many parameters.

The Hammond organ (1935) is an electromechanical additive synthesizer:
tonewheels generate sine-like partials, and drawbars set the amplitude
of each. Nine drawbars = nine harmonics = a playable parameterization
of a useful subspace of periodic waveforms.

** FM synthesis (Chowning / Yamaha DX7)

Discovered by John Chowning at Stanford (1967): use one sine oscillator
(the modulator) to vary the frequency of another (the carrier).

    output(t) = sin(2*pi*fc*t + I * sin(2*pi*fm*t))

Three parameters---carrier frequency fc, modulator frequency fm,
and modulation index I---produce a huge range of spectra. When fm/fc
is a simple ratio, you get harmonic spectra; irrational ratios give
inharmonic, bell-like timbres.

The Yamaha DX7 (1983) used 6 operators (sine oscillators) arranged in
32 fixed "algorithms" (routing topologies). This is cheap to compute
(just sine lookups and addition) but notoriously hard to program
intuitively, because the relationship between parameters and resulting
timbre is highly nonlinear.

FM synthesis generates waveforms from a dynamical process (one oscillator
driving another), not from a static formula. The modulation index is a
natural parameter to vary over time for timbral animation.

** TODO huh ? Phase distortion synthesis (Casio CZ)

Casio's 1984 workaround for Yamaha's FM patent. Instead of modulating
frequency, you distort the /phase/ of a cosine lookup:

- A linear phase ramp through a cosine table gives a plain cosine.
- A piecewise-linear phase function (fast then slow, or vice versa)
  gives a distorted waveform with different harmonic content.

Different phase-distortion curves produce sawtooth-like, pulse-like, and
resonant waveforms, all from a single cosine table. The CZ series had 8
basic waveforms, including three with simulated filter resonance: these
used a /windowed hard-sync/ technique, where a sine at the resonant
frequency is amplitude-shaped by a window function at the fundamental.

This is a clever basis: {cosine} x {space of phase-distortion curves}.
One or two parameters control the distortion shape, giving timbral
variation cheaply.

** TODO huh ? Waveshaping synthesis (Chebyshev / Buchla)

Feed a simple waveform (usually sine) through a nonlinear transfer
function:

    output(t) = f(input(t))

where f is a static function (a curve). As noted above, Chebyshev
polynomials let you design f to produce specific harmonics.

Don Buchla's "West Coast" synthesis (1960s onward) is built on this
idea. Instead of filtering a rich waveform (Moog's approach), Buchla
/adds/ harmonics to a simple waveform by running it through a
wavefolder---a circuit that "folds" the waveform back on itself when
it exceeds a threshold. Increasing the input amplitude drives the
signal further into the fold, adding more harmonics.

The "basis" here is: {input waveform} x {waveshaping function} x
{input amplitude}. The amplitude acts as a timbral control, which is
musically interesting because dynamics and timbre become coupled.

** Wavetable and vector synthesis

Store many single-cycle waveforms in a table. Scan through the table
over time, crossfading between adjacent waveforms:

- PPG Wave (late 1970s, Wolfgang Palm): 32 wavetables of 64 waveforms
  each. No interpolation---audible stepping between waveforms.
- Sequential Prophet VS (1986): 2D interpolation between 4 waveform
  sources using a joystick ("vector synthesis").
- Modern wavetable synths (Serum, Vital): smooth interpolation with
  antialiasing.

The "basis" is a discrete set of stored waveforms with interpolation.
The quality depends entirely on the choice of waveforms in the table.
Computationally trivial (table lookup + interpolation).

* Band-limiting

A practical concern for digital synthesis: naive generation of waveforms
with discontinuities (sawtooth, square, pulse) causes aliasing. Several
algorithms fix this:

- BLIT (band-limited impulse train): generate a stream of sinc-shaped
  pulses, then integrate to get sawtooth, square, etc.
- PolyBLEP (polynomial band-limited step): correct the discontinuity
  region using a cheap polynomial, avoiding both aliasing and lookup
  tables. Only a few arithmetic operations per sample.

These are less "bases" than practical implementations of the classic
waveforms that sound clean at any frequency.

** TODO The filter-as-oscillator

A resonant filter with high Q, excited by an impulse or noise, /is/ an
oscillator. The state-variable filter has an internal state that, at
self-oscillation, traces a limit cycle. This is how many analog synths
generate sine waves: crank up the filter resonance until it self-
oscillates.

This connects to the dynamical-systems perspective: a resonant filter
is a damped harmonic oscillator. At self-oscillation, it's an undamped
one. Add nonlinearity (saturation, clipping) and you get more complex
limit cycles with richer spectra.

** TODO Physical modeling as waveform generation

Karplus-Strong is the simplest case, but the idea generalizes to
digital waveguide synthesis (Julius O. Smith): simulate the physics
of vibrating strings, air columns, membranes, and bars using delay
lines, filters, and scattering junctions.

The "basis" here isn't a set of mathematical functions---it's the space
of physical systems. Parameters are physical: string length, tension,
stiffness, pick position, bow pressure. The waveform emerges from
simulation rather than formula.

** TODO huh ? Formant-wave-function (FOF) synthesis

Developed at IRCAM by Xavier Rodet. Each "grain" is a damped sinusoid
windowed by an envelope, tuned to a formant frequency. Multiple grains
at different formant frequencies are triggered synchronously at the
fundamental pitch rate.

The basis here is: {damped sinusoids at formant frequencies} x
{window functions} x {fundamental period}. It's particularly good for
voice synthesis.

* What seems most promising for cheap, flexible exploration

1. *Subtractive with parameterized source*: Combine a parameterized
   waveform (e.g., trapezoid) with a cheap digital filter (biquad,
   state-variable, one-pole). Two parameters on the source + two on
   the filter = huge timbral range. This is what most analog synths do.

2. *Van der Pol or other nonlinear oscillators*: A single-parameter ODE
   that morphs from sine to square is conceptually elegant and has
   "organic" intermediate shapes. Could be combined with a filter.

3. *Waveshaping with amplitude control*: A fixed nonlinear function
   (wavefolder, tanh, Chebyshev sum) applied to a sine, with input
   gain as the timbral parameter. Very cheap and highly interactive.

4. *Coupled magic-circle oscillators*: Two cheap sine oscillators
   with some coupling term (FM, AM, ring mod). A handful of parameters,
   and the interaction produces evolving spectra.

5. *Karplus-Strong variants*: Replace the noise burst with a shaped
   initial condition, vary the filter, and you get a family of plucked/
   struck/bowed timbres from a very simple algorithm.
